# Chapter 03. Learning in the Brain

## 03.00 Brain Learning Overview

![](images/image1.png){width="7.268055555555556in" height="7.268055555555556in"}

Brain learning is the set of biological processes through which experience produces lasting changes in neural activity, connectivity, internal representations, and behavior. Rather than operating as a single learning algorithm, the brain combines multiple mechanisms distributed across cortical and subcortical systems. These mechanisms allow organisms to acquire sensory regularities, predict events, remember experiences, develop skills, evaluate outcomes, and continuously adapt actions to changing environments.

At the cellular level, learning depends strongly on neural plasticity, the capacity of neurons and synapses to modify their functional relationships as activity unfolds. Changes in synaptic strength can alter how effectively signals propagate through a circuit, gradually reshaping future responses to similar inputs. Learning therefore does not simply store information in isolated locations; it modifies distributed patterns of interaction across populations of neurons that participate in perception, memory, prediction, and action.

A fundamental principle of biological learning is that relationships between neural events matter. When neurons repeatedly participate in related activity, their connections may be strengthened, weakened, stabilized, or reorganized. Hebbian learning captures an important part of this idea by associating correlated neural activity with changes in connectivity, while spike-timing-dependent plasticity extends it by emphasizing the temporal order and precise timing of neuronal firing. Together, these mechanisms illustrate how local interactions can gradually construct useful representations.

Learning in the brain is also deeply temporal. Neural systems must distinguish events that occur together accidentally from relationships that reliably unfold across milliseconds, seconds, minutes, or much longer periods. Temporal structure allows the brain to learn sequences, anticipate consequences, associate actions with later outcomes, and identify regularities in changing sensory streams. This sensitivity to time is essential for motor control, language, navigation, episodic memory, and prediction in dynamic environments.

Not all learning can be explained by correlations between neural signals. Organisms must also determine whether actions produce desirable or undesirable consequences. Reinforcement-related mechanisms provide this capability by connecting behavior with reward, punishment, motivation, and expectation. Neural circuits involving the basal ganglia and neuromodulatory systems contribute to adjusting future action selection according to experienced outcomes, creating a biological foundation for adaptive decision making and reinforcement learning.

Dopamine plays an especially important role in this process, although its function is broader than a simple representation of pleasure or reward. Dopaminergic activity is closely associated with differences between expected and obtained outcomes, often described computationally through reward prediction errors. When outcomes are better or worse than anticipated, these signals can influence plasticity and update expectations, allowing behavior to improve through repeated interaction with the environment.

Different brain systems contribute complementary forms of learning rather than performing identical computations. The hippocampus is strongly associated with rapid acquisition of episodic and relational information, enabling experiences, contexts, locations, and events to be linked after relatively limited exposure. Cortical systems generally support slower extraction and stabilization of statistical regularities across repeated experiences, producing increasingly generalized representations that can remain useful beyond the circumstances in which individual memories were originally formed.

This division creates an important balance between rapid learning and stable knowledge. A system that changes too quickly may overwrite previously acquired information, whereas one that changes too slowly may fail to adapt when conditions change. Interactions between hippocampal and cortical systems provide a biological perspective on this stability--plasticity problem. Processes such as consolidation and replay can repeatedly reactivate experiences, allowing newly acquired information to become integrated with established representations over longer timescales.

Brain learning is therefore inherently continual. Animals do not normally complete a fixed training phase and then permanently stop learning. New sensory evidence, actions, rewards, social interactions, and environmental changes continuously modify internal models while previously useful knowledge must remain accessible. This creates the biological counterpart of the continual-learning problem in artificial intelligence, including interference between old and new knowledge and the possibility that later learning can weaken earlier memories.

Forgetting is consequently not always a failure of biological memory. Selective weakening, interference, reconsolidation, and restructuring can help neural systems remain adaptable by reducing obsolete information and emphasizing patterns that continue to matter. Effective learning requires a balance among retention, modification, and forgetting. This differs from treating memory as a static database: biological knowledge remains embedded within an adaptive system whose representations can change whenever new evidence alters what should be predicted or remembered.

Learning also occurs at multiple spatial scales. Synaptic modifications influence individual connections, local circuits organize recurring patterns, specialized brain regions support particular computational functions, and large-scale networks coordinate perception, memory, valuation, attention, and action. These levels interact continuously. A behavioral adaptation observed at the organism level may therefore emerge from many smaller modifications distributed across different neural systems rather than from a single identifiable change.

Another defining property is that biological learning operates within closed perception--action loops. The brain does not merely receive a prepared dataset. Actions determine what sensory information becomes available, sensory consequences modify internal states, predictions guide subsequent behavior, and resulting outcomes provide additional learning signals. Learning is therefore inseparable from active interaction with the world, particularly in navigation, manipulation, exploration, motor control, and other forms of embodied intelligence.

The brain also learns under conditions radically different from conventional machine-learning pipelines. Biological systems receive noisy, incomplete, multimodal, temporally correlated data and rarely obtain explicit labels for every observation. Nevertheless, they discover useful regularities through observation, prediction, interaction, internal feedback, social information, and sparse external rewards. This combination suggests that intelligence depends on integrating several learning signals rather than relying exclusively on fully supervised examples.

From an AI perspective, brain learning should therefore be viewed as a family of complementary computational principles rather than as a biological version of backpropagation. Synaptic plasticity suggests mechanisms for adaptive connections; Hebbian learning emphasizes correlation; STDP introduces temporal locality; reward-related systems motivate reinforcement learning; hippocampal processes illuminate rapid memory formation; and cortical learning provides models for gradual abstraction, generalization, and long-term knowledge organization.

Biological learning and artificial backpropagation nevertheless should not be treated as direct equivalents. Backpropagation efficiently computes gradients through explicitly defined computational graphs and objective functions, whereas biological neurons learn through local activity, neuromodulation, recurrent interactions, structural constraints, and mechanisms distributed across multiple timescales. The comparison is valuable because it exposes both the effectiveness of engineered optimization and the unresolved question of how brains achieve powerful learning without implementing conventional backpropagation in its standard form.

These differences become particularly important when considering future adaptive AI. Artificial systems increasingly require continual learning, memory consolidation, sparse feedback, self-supervised representation learning, embodied interaction, and adaptation without catastrophic loss of previous capabilities. Neuroscience does not provide a ready-made engineering blueprint, but it offers experimentally grounded examples of systems that combine these properties within a single continuously operating organism.

The study of brain learning therefore forms a bridge between neurons and higher intelligence. Synaptic plasticity explains how experience can modify circuits; Hebbian and timing-dependent mechanisms describe local adaptation; reinforcement systems connect predictions and outcomes; hippocampal and cortical systems reveal complementary learning timescales; and continual learning exposes the challenge of preserving useful knowledge while remaining adaptable. These themes provide the foundation for the subsequent study of learning mechanisms in the brain and their possible implications for artificial intelligence.

## 03.01 Synaptic Plasticity [w/Code]

![](images/image2.png){width="7.268055555555556in" height="7.268055555555556in"}

Synaptic plasticity is the capacity of synapses to change their functional strength, efficiency, structure, or signaling properties as a consequence of neural activity and experience. It is one of the fundamental biological mechanisms through which the brain learns, stores information, adapts to changing environments, and reorganizes behavior. These changes may occur over milliseconds, minutes, hours, days, or much longer periods, depending on the underlying neural process.

A synapse is not a fixed communication channel between neurons. Its effectiveness can vary according to previous activity, the timing of presynaptic and postsynaptic firing, neuromodulatory signals, receptor availability, and structural changes around the synaptic connection. Because neural circuits contain enormous numbers of such adaptable connections, local synaptic modifications can collectively reshape the information-processing properties of entire networks and influence perception, memory, and action.

Synaptic plasticity can broadly produce either strengthening or weakening of communication between neurons. When repeated activity increases the effectiveness of transmission, the process may support stronger associations between particular neural patterns. When transmission becomes weaker, previously dominant relationships can be reduced or reorganized. Learning therefore depends not only on strengthening useful connections but also on selectively weakening connections that are less informative, outdated, or inconsistent with new experience.

Short-term plasticity changes synaptic effectiveness over relatively brief timescales, often ranging from milliseconds to several seconds or minutes. Facilitation can temporarily increase synaptic transmission when closely spaced neural events enhance transmitter release, whereas depression can temporarily decrease transmission when available resources become depleted. These mechanisms allow synapses to dynamically filter temporal information and influence how neural circuits respond to rapidly changing patterns of activity.

Long-term potentiation, commonly abbreviated as LTP, describes a persistent increase in synaptic strength following particular patterns of neural activation. LTP has been extensively associated with learning and memory because it demonstrates how transient neural activity can produce durable changes in communication between neurons. In many studied circuits, repeated or coordinated activation triggers molecular processes that increase postsynaptic responsiveness and can eventually alter the physical organization of synaptic connections.

Long-term depression, or LTD, represents a persistent reduction in synaptic efficacy produced by particular activity patterns. LTD complements potentiation by providing a mechanism for weakening connections rather than continuously increasing their strength. This balance is essential because a neural system in which every active synapse only became stronger would eventually lose useful selectivity. Potentiation and depression together help neural networks maintain flexible, differentiated, and adaptive representations.

At excitatory synapses, plasticity frequently involves changes in receptor dynamics and intracellular signaling. Activity can influence the number, sensitivity, or distribution of receptors located on the postsynaptic membrane, thereby modifying how strongly the postsynaptic neuron responds to future input. Calcium-dependent signaling pathways are especially important in many experimentally studied forms of plasticity because intracellular calcium can trigger biochemical cascades associated with both strengthening and weakening.

Structural plasticity extends learning beyond changes in signaling efficiency. Dendritic spines can grow, shrink, stabilize, or disappear, while new synaptic contacts may form and existing ones may be eliminated. These structural changes show that learning can physically reorganize neural circuitry. Functional adaptation and structural modification are therefore closely connected, allowing frequently used pathways to become stabilized while other connections remain flexible or are gradually removed.

Synaptic plasticity is strongly influenced by the temporal relationship between neural events. The relative timing of activity in connected neurons can determine whether a synapse is strengthened or weakened. This principle becomes particularly explicit in spike-timing-dependent plasticity, where changes depend on the order and interval between presynaptic and postsynaptic spikes. Such timing sensitivity provides a biological mechanism for learning temporal relationships, sequences, and possible causal structure.

Plasticity is also regulated by neuromodulatory systems rather than depending entirely on local electrical activity. Dopamine, acetylcholine, norepinephrine, serotonin, and other modulatory signals can alter whether particular activity patterns lead to lasting synaptic change. This allows learning to depend on behavioral context, reward, novelty, attention, uncertainty, or arousal. The same sensory event may therefore produce different amounts of learning depending on the internal state and significance of the experience.

Another important concept is homeostatic plasticity, which helps neural systems maintain stable levels of activity despite continual local changes. If synaptic strengthening occurred without regulation, neural circuits could become excessively excitable, while unrestricted weakening could make them unresponsive. Homeostatic mechanisms adjust synaptic or cellular responsiveness to preserve operating ranges, creating a balance between flexibility and stability that is essential for long-term learning.

Synaptic plasticity should therefore be understood as a family of interacting mechanisms rather than as a single universal learning rule. Different brain regions, cell types, receptors, developmental stages, and behavioral contexts can express different forms of plasticity. Some mechanisms support rapid adaptation, others support long-term memory, and others regulate network stability. Their interaction enables the brain to learn continuously while preserving enough structure to retain previously useful knowledge.

The hippocampus provides an important example because synaptic plasticity within hippocampal circuits has been extensively investigated in relation to episodic and spatial memory. Rapid changes in synaptic strength can support the formation of associations among events, places, and contexts after relatively limited experience. Cortical learning can then operate across longer timescales, allowing repeated patterns to become progressively integrated into more stable and generalized representations.

Plasticity also contributes to adaptation in sensory and motor systems. Repeated sensory experience can modify neural responses to frequently encountered features, while motor practice can strengthen and reorganize circuits involved in movement planning and execution. After injury or altered sensory input, plastic mechanisms may permit partial functional reorganization. Thus, synaptic plasticity supports not only explicit memory but also perception, skill acquisition, behavioral adaptation, and recovery.

From an artificial intelligence perspective, synaptic plasticity provides a biological example of learning through distributed parameter adaptation. Artificial neural networks similarly modify connection weights to improve performance, but biological synapses operate through local biochemical and electrical processes rather than conventional global gradient computation. This comparison is useful because it highlights possible alternatives involving local learning signals, sparse updates, temporal learning, and activity-dependent adaptation.

Biological plasticity also differs from typical machine learning because learning remains active during normal operation. The brain does not usually separate experience into rigid training and inference phases. Perception, action, reward, memory formation, and synaptic modification can occur within the same continuous process. This property is especially relevant to continual learning systems and embodied AI, where models must adapt to new situations while operating in dynamic environments without repeatedly retraining from scratch.

However, unrestricted plasticity creates serious problems. If new experiences rapidly overwrite established synaptic patterns, previously learned information may be degraded. Biological systems therefore require mechanisms that determine when connections should remain stable and when they should become modifiable. This stability--plasticity balance is closely related to catastrophic forgetting in artificial neural networks and motivates research into consolidation, replay, selective adaptation, and protected representations.

Synaptic plasticity forms the foundation for several learning mechanisms developed in subsequent sections of this chapter. Hebbian learning emphasizes correlated activity, spike-timing-dependent plasticity introduces precise temporal relationships, reinforcement-related mechanisms connect synaptic change with reward and prediction error, and continual learning examines how new adaptation can coexist with preserved knowledge. Together, these mechanisms explain how local changes at synapses can scale toward memory, prediction, adaptive behavior, and intelligent learning.

## 03.02 Hebbian Learning [w/Code]

![](images/image3.png){width="7.268055555555556in" height="7.268055555555556in"}

Hebbian learning is a foundational principle for understanding how neural connections can change through correlated activity. The central idea is that when two neurons are repeatedly active together, the synaptic connection between them can become stronger. This provides a biologically plausible explanation for how repeated experience can gradually reshape neural circuits and create associations among sensory patterns, internal states, memories, and actions.

The concept is commonly summarized by the phrase "cells that fire together wire together," although the full biological reality is more complex. If activity in a presynaptic neuron repeatedly contributes to activation of a postsynaptic neuron, the connection between them may become more effective. Through repeated co-activation, neural networks can increase the probability that related patterns will activate each other again in the future.

Hebbian learning is fundamentally local because synaptic modification can depend on signals available near the connection itself. Unlike conventional backpropagation in artificial neural networks, it does not require a global error gradient to be calculated across many layers. Instead, changes may arise from relationships between presynaptic activity, postsynaptic activity, and local biochemical conditions at individual synapses.

A simple computational representation of Hebbian learning expresses the change in synaptic weight as proportional to the product of presynaptic and postsynaptic activity. When both neurons are strongly active, the connection tends to increase. If one or both neurons are inactive, little strengthening occurs. This basic rule demonstrates how repeated statistical correlations in experience can become encoded directly into connection strengths.

Because of this correlation-sensitive property, Hebbian learning can support feature discovery without explicit labels. Neurons that repeatedly respond to related sensory inputs may become increasingly connected, causing frequently occurring patterns to form stable internal representations. In this sense, Hebbian mechanisms resemble unsupervised or self-organizing learning because useful structure can emerge from the statistics of incoming activity rather than from externally provided target values.

Hebbian learning is particularly important for understanding associative memory. When multiple neural representations are activated together, their connections can be strengthened so that later activation of only part of the pattern may help reactivate the remaining components. This principle provides an intuitive mechanism for linking objects with contexts, events with locations, sensory cues with memories, and actions with frequently associated situations.

At the network level, repeated Hebbian modification can produce groups of neurons that are strongly interconnected. Such coordinated groups are sometimes described as cell assemblies, representing distributed patterns that respond collectively to particular features or experiences. Learning therefore does not necessarily require a single neuron to store an entire concept; information can instead be represented through patterns of connectivity distributed across many neurons.

Pure Hebbian strengthening, however, creates an important stability problem. If every correlated activation continuously increased synaptic weights, connections could grow without limit, eventually reducing network selectivity and producing unstable activity. Biological learning therefore requires regulatory processes that normalize, constrain, or counterbalance Hebbian strengthening. Synaptic weakening and homeostatic mechanisms help preserve useful competition among neural representations.

Competition is important because neural systems must distinguish informative patterns rather than simply strengthen every frequently active pathway. When some synaptic connections become stronger, others may remain unchanged or become weaker, allowing neural circuits to specialize. This competitive process can produce selective representations in which neurons become increasingly responsive to recurring combinations of inputs while becoming less responsive to unrelated activity.

Hebbian learning also interacts with synaptic plasticity mechanisms operating at different timescales. Long-term potentiation can provide persistent strengthening of synapses following coordinated activity, while long-term depression can reduce connections under other activity conditions. These complementary mechanisms allow correlation-based learning to operate within a broader system that supports both memory formation and adaptive restructuring of neural networks.

Temporal order adds another level of precision to Hebbian principles. Simple correlation indicates that neurons are active together, but biological learning may also depend on which neuron fires first and how much time separates their activity. Spike-timing-dependent plasticity extends Hebbian learning by using the relative timing of presynaptic and postsynaptic spikes to determine whether a connection should strengthen or weaken.

Hebbian mechanisms alone also do not indicate whether a learned association is useful, rewarding, or behaviorally important. Neuromodulatory signals can provide this additional context. Dopamine and other modulators may influence whether correlated neural activity produces lasting synaptic modification, allowing local Hebbian relationships to interact with reward, novelty, attention, motivation, or behavioral significance.

This combination suggests that biological learning is not simply correlation detection. Correlation provides information about which neural events occur together, while reinforcement-related signals can indicate which relationships matter for future behavior. Together, these mechanisms allow the brain to integrate environmental regularities with consequences, creating representations that reflect both statistical structure and behavioral value.

Hebbian learning also offers an important perspective on cortical representation learning. Repeated exposure to structured sensory environments creates correlations among visual, auditory, spatial, and sensorimotor signals. Local learning mechanisms can gradually transform these recurring relationships into organized neural representations, helping circuits become specialized for features, combinations of features, and regularities that frequently appear in the environment.

From an artificial intelligence perspective, Hebbian learning provides an alternative model of adaptation based on local interactions rather than globally propagated errors. Artificial Hebbian rules can update weights using neuron activations and can support associative memory, competitive learning, feature extraction, and self-organization. Such approaches are especially relevant when researchers investigate biologically plausible learning or systems that must adapt continuously during operation.

However, Hebbian learning and modern deep learning should not be considered equivalent. Backpropagation explicitly optimizes a defined objective by propagating error information through a computational network, whereas Hebbian learning primarily strengthens statistical relationships among active units. Backpropagation can efficiently coordinate large numbers of parameters toward a global task objective, while Hebbian rules emphasize locality, correlation, and decentralized adaptation.

The contrast between these approaches raises an important question for brain-inspired AI: whether powerful learning systems can combine global optimization with local adaptation. Hybrid approaches may use conventional gradient-based learning for large-scale representation formation while adding local plasticity for continual adaptation, memory updates, or context-sensitive behavior. Such combinations could provide greater flexibility than either mechanism alone.

Hebbian learning therefore forms an essential bridge between synaptic plasticity and more advanced neural learning mechanisms. It explains how repeated co-activation can strengthen associations, how distributed representations may emerge from experience, and why competition and stabilization are necessary. It also provides the conceptual foundation for spike-timing-dependent plasticity, associative memory, continual learning, and biologically inspired approaches to adaptive artificial intelligence.

## 03.03 STDP [w/Code]

![](images/image4.png){width="7.268055555555556in" height="7.268055555555556in"}

Spike-timing-dependent plasticity, or STDP, is a form of synaptic plasticity in which changes in synaptic strength depend on the precise timing between presynaptic and postsynaptic spikes. Rather than treating neural activity only as a measure of how strongly two neurons are activated, STDP incorporates temporal order. It therefore extends correlation-based learning by asking not only whether neurons fire together, but also which neuron fires first and how closely their spikes occur in time.

The basic STDP principle can be understood through the relative timing between a presynaptic spike and a postsynaptic spike. When the presynaptic neuron fires shortly before the postsynaptic neuron, the connection is often strengthened. When the postsynaptic neuron fires before the presynaptic neuron, the same connection may instead weaken. This temporal asymmetry provides a local mechanism through which neural circuits can become sensitive to ordered relationships among events.

A common notation defines the timing difference as Δt between postsynaptic and presynaptic firing. Positive timing differences, where presynaptic activity precedes postsynaptic firing, are frequently associated with long-term potentiation. Negative timing differences can be associated with long-term depression. The magnitude of the synaptic modification typically decreases as the time interval between the two spikes becomes larger, producing a characteristic temporal learning window.

This learning window is central to STDP because it determines how strongly different spike relationships influence synaptic change. Spikes separated by only a few milliseconds may produce substantial modification, whereas widely separated spikes may have much weaker effects. STDP therefore acts as a temporally selective learning mechanism that emphasizes events occurring within biologically meaningful intervals rather than treating all correlations as equally important.

STDP can be interpreted as an extension of Hebbian learning. Classical Hebbian reasoning emphasizes that correlated activity can strengthen connections, but STDP introduces temporal direction into that relationship. If one neuron consistently fires before another, the first neuron may contribute to predicting or causing the second neuron's activation. This makes STDP particularly relevant for learning sequences, transitions, sensorimotor relationships, and temporally ordered patterns.

At the synaptic level, STDP arises through interactions among membrane depolarization, neurotransmitter release, receptor activation, and intracellular signaling. Calcium dynamics are particularly important in many studied forms of STDP because the amount and timing of calcium entry into the postsynaptic neuron can influence whether biochemical pathways favor potentiation or depression. Thus, millisecond-scale spike timing can trigger molecular processes that produce much longer-lasting changes in synaptic efficacy.

STDP is a local learning mechanism because the necessary information can be available near the synapse itself. Presynaptic terminals provide information about incoming spikes, postsynaptic activity provides information about the receiving neuron, and intracellular biochemical processes determine the resulting modification. No explicit global error signal is required. This locality makes STDP attractive as a biological model of decentralized learning and as an inspiration for neuromorphic computation.

The temporal sensitivity of STDP allows neural circuits to encode regularities in continuously changing sensory streams. If a particular sensory feature reliably precedes another, connections from neurons representing the earlier feature to neurons representing the later feature can be selectively modified. Over repeated experiences, such mechanisms may contribute to the learning of temporal sequences and to the prediction of events that commonly follow one another.

STDP is also relevant to sensorimotor learning because actions and their sensory consequences occur in ordered sequences. Neural activity associated with a movement may repeatedly precede activity associated with the resulting sensory feedback. Timing-dependent plasticity can therefore contribute to circuits that connect motor commands, predicted consequences, and observed outcomes. These relationships are important for adaptive control, coordination, and the formation of internal predictive models.

Although the simple pre-before-post strengthening and post-before-pre weakening rule is widely used to explain STDP, biological STDP is more diverse. The exact learning rule can differ across brain regions, neuron types, synaptic locations, firing rates, and experimental conditions. Some synapses show different temporal windows or nonlinear interactions among multiple spikes. STDP should therefore be understood as a family of timing-sensitive plasticity mechanisms rather than one universal mathematical rule.

Repeated spike patterns can also interact in ways that cannot be fully explained by isolated pairs of spikes. Triplets, bursts, firing frequency, dendritic location, and recent synaptic history may influence the resulting plasticity. This means that biological learning depends on richer temporal context than a simple two-spike relationship suggests. More advanced models of STDP incorporate these additional factors to better represent the dynamics observed in neural circuits.

Neuromodulation further changes how STDP operates. Dopamine, acetylcholine, norepinephrine, and other modulatory signals can influence whether timing relationships lead to durable plasticity. This allows the brain to combine precise local timing with broader information about reward, attention, novelty, motivation, or behavioral relevance. A temporal association that occurs during an important event may therefore be learned differently from the same association occurring in an irrelevant context.

This interaction is especially significant for reinforcement learning in the brain. A neural event and its behavioral consequence may be separated by more time than the narrow STDP window can directly connect. Temporary synaptic traces, sometimes described conceptually as eligibility traces, can preserve information about recent neural interactions until a later reward-related signal arrives. Neuromodulation can then reinforce or weaken selected connections based on both timing and outcome.

STDP also contributes to competition and organization within neural networks. Synapses that consistently participate in predictive or well-timed activity patterns may become stronger, while poorly timed or less useful connections may weaken. Over time, this can produce selective pathways and structured representations. Such competitive temporal learning can help networks organize themselves according to recurring sequences rather than merely according to static patterns of co-activation.

From the perspective of memory, STDP provides a mechanism for encoding the order of events rather than only their simultaneous association. Many experiences are inherently sequential: sounds unfold over time, movements consist of ordered actions, and episodes contain sequences of events. Timing-dependent plasticity can support neural representations that preserve these relationships, contributing to sequence memory, temporal prediction, and the organization of episodic experience.

STDP has strong relevance to spiking neural networks because these models represent information using discrete spikes and their timing. Artificial STDP rules can modify synaptic weights directly from spike relationships, allowing networks to learn without conventional backpropagation. Such systems have been explored for feature learning, temporal pattern recognition, event-based sensing, adaptive control, and other tasks where precise timing naturally carries useful information.

Neuromorphic computing provides another important connection. Hardware designed around event-driven processing can use spike-based representations that activate computation only when events occur. STDP-like local learning rules can potentially update connections close to where signals are processed, reducing dependence on centralized gradient calculation and large memory transfers. These properties make STDP relevant to research on low-power, adaptive, and continuously learning computing systems.

However, STDP alone does not provide the task-level optimization capabilities of modern deep learning. It does not naturally specify how millions or billions of parameters should cooperate to minimize a complex global objective. Instead, its strength lies in temporal locality, biological plausibility, event-driven adaptation, and the ability to learn relationships from ongoing activity. These differences suggest that STDP and gradient-based learning address different aspects of intelligent adaptation.

Hybrid systems may therefore combine global optimization with local timing-dependent plasticity. A network could be pretrained using backpropagation to acquire broad representations, while STDP-like mechanisms adapt selected connections during real-time interaction. Such an architecture could support fast personalization, sensor adaptation, continual learning, or changing environmental conditions without requiring complete retraining whenever new temporal patterns are encountered.

STDP forms an important bridge between synaptic plasticity, Hebbian learning, reinforcement mechanisms, and brain-inspired computation. It shows how the precise order of neural events can influence connectivity and how temporal relationships can become embedded in neural circuits. By connecting timing, local plasticity, prediction, sequence learning, and neuromodulation, STDP provides a key biological principle for understanding adaptive intelligence in both brains and artificial systems.

## 03.04 Reinforcement Learning in the Brain [w/Code]

![](images/image5.png){width="7.268055555555556in" height="7.268055555555556in"}

Reinforcement learning in the brain refers to the process by which behavior is gradually shaped through consequences. Actions that lead to beneficial outcomes become more likely to be repeated, while actions associated with unfavorable outcomes can become less likely. Rather than relying on explicit labels or instructions, the nervous system learns from interaction, feedback, reward, punishment, expectation, and changes in internal physiological state.

A central challenge in reinforcement learning is action selection. At any moment, an organism may have several possible behaviors available, each associated with uncertain consequences. Neural systems must therefore evaluate alternatives, select one action, observe the resulting outcome, and modify future preferences. This creates a closed learning loop in which perception, valuation, decision, action, and feedback continuously influence one another.

The basal ganglia are strongly associated with this type of learning because they participate in action selection, habit formation, motivation, and value-based behavior. Cortical regions provide information about goals, context, sensory features, and possible actions, while basal ganglia circuits help determine which behaviors should be facilitated or suppressed. Their interaction allows previously experienced outcomes to influence future decisions in similar situations.

Reinforcement learning depends not only on the reward that is actually received, but also on what was expected before the outcome occurred. If an outcome is better than predicted, learning should increase the value assigned to preceding actions or states. If it is worse than predicted, those expectations should be reduced. This difference between expected and obtained outcomes provides a powerful signal for updating behavior and internal value estimates.

Dopamine is closely associated with these learning processes, particularly with signals related to reward prediction error. Dopaminergic activity can influence synaptic plasticity in circuits involved in value learning and action selection. When outcomes differ from expectations, dopamine-related signals can modify the strength of relevant connections, thereby changing how strongly particular actions, cues, or states influence future behavior.

This mechanism resembles temporal-difference learning in artificial reinforcement learning. Instead of waiting until the end of an entire behavioral episode, the system can update expectations whenever new information changes the predicted value of the future. Successive estimates therefore propagate information backward through experience, allowing earlier states and actions to acquire value because they reliably lead toward later rewards or away from undesirable outcomes.

Reward in biological systems is broader than a simple external prize. Food, safety, social interaction, novelty, relief from discomfort, successful goal completion, and changes in internal physiological conditions can all influence behavior. The effective learning signal therefore depends on the organism's current needs and context. A consequence that is valuable in one state may be relatively unimportant in another, making biological reinforcement strongly state-dependent.

Punishment and negative outcomes also contribute to adaptive behavior, but they are not merely the opposite of reward in every neural mechanism. Different circuits participate in aversion, threat detection, pain, avoidance, and behavioral inhibition. Reinforcement learning in the brain therefore involves multiple interacting systems that evaluate both desirable and undesirable consequences while balancing approach, avoidance, exploration, and behavioral control.

An important issue is the exploration--exploitation tradeoff. An organism can exploit actions that have previously produced good outcomes, but it must also explore alternatives because environments change and existing knowledge may be incomplete. Excessive exploitation can trap behavior in suboptimal routines, while excessive exploration can be inefficient or dangerous. Neural systems must dynamically regulate this balance according to uncertainty, novelty, risk, and previous experience.

Learning also depends on credit assignment. Rewards often occur after a delay, making it difficult to determine which earlier actions were responsible for the eventual outcome. Neural systems require mechanisms that preserve information about recently active states, actions, and synapses until the consequence becomes known. Eligibility-like traces and neuromodulatory signals provide one conceptual explanation for how delayed outcomes can selectively influence recently involved neural pathways.

Reinforcement mechanisms interact closely with synaptic plasticity, Hebbian learning, and spike-timing-dependent plasticity. Correlated activity can identify which neural elements participate together, timing can indicate the order of events, and reward-related modulation can determine which of those relationships are behaviorally valuable. Biological learning therefore combines local activity patterns with global or semi-global signals reflecting the significance of an outcome.

The cortex, basal ganglia, hippocampus, and other systems provide complementary information during reinforcement learning. The cortex can represent sensory states, goals, and task structure; the hippocampus can encode episodes and contextual relationships; and basal ganglia circuits can learn values and action tendencies. By integrating current perception with remembered experience and expected outcomes, the brain can make decisions that are sensitive to both immediate and long-term consequences.

Reinforcement learning also contributes to the gradual transition from deliberate behavior to habits. Early in learning, actions may require substantial attention and evaluation of consequences. With repeated successful experience, action sequences can become increasingly automatic and triggered by familiar contexts. This improves efficiency, but it can also make behavior less flexible when environmental conditions change and previously learned habits are no longer appropriate.

Goal-directed and habitual control therefore represent complementary modes rather than completely separate systems. Goal-directed behavior evaluates expected outcomes and can adapt rapidly when values change, whereas habitual behavior relies more strongly on learned stimulus--response or state--action relationships. Adaptive intelligence requires mechanisms that determine when efficient habits are sufficient and when more flexible evaluation or planning is necessary.

From an artificial intelligence perspective, brain reinforcement learning provides an important biological parallel to agent--environment interaction. Artificial agents observe states, select actions, receive rewards, and update policies or value functions. Biological systems perform analogous functions, but they rely on distributed neural circuits, neuromodulation, multiple memory systems, internal drives, and continual learning rather than on a single explicitly defined reward function.

This difference is important because biological reward signals are often sparse, uncertain, context-dependent, and influenced by multiple competing objectives. The brain must learn while simultaneously perceiving, acting, remembering, regulating the body, and adapting to changing conditions. These properties motivate AI research on intrinsic motivation, hierarchical reinforcement learning, model-based reinforcement learning, continual adaptation, and embodied agents that learn through long-term interaction.

Reinforcement learning in the brain therefore links neural plasticity with adaptive behavior. Synaptic mechanisms provide the capacity to change, dopamine-related signals help represent differences between expected and obtained outcomes, basal ganglia circuits contribute to value learning and action selection, and cortical and hippocampal systems provide context, memory, and goals. Together, these processes explain how experience can progressively transform consequences into future behavioral preferences.

## 03.05 Dopamine and Reward Prediction [w/Code]

![](images/image6.png){width="7.268055555555556in" height="7.268055555555556in"}

Dopamine is a neuromodulator that plays a central role in learning, motivation, action selection, and the evaluation of outcomes. Although it is often described casually as a "reward chemical," its function is more accurately understood as part of a distributed system that signals changes in expected value, behavioral relevance, and learning opportunity. Dopamine helps the brain adjust future behavior by linking outcomes with the states, cues, and actions that preceded them.

A key concept in dopamine-based learning is reward prediction. The brain does not respond only to rewards after they occur; it also learns to anticipate them from earlier cues and situations. When a stimulus reliably predicts a valuable outcome, neural activity can gradually shift from the reward itself toward the predictive cue. This allows organisms to prepare behavior in advance rather than reacting only after consequences have already occurred.

Reward prediction error describes the difference between the reward that was expected and the outcome that was actually obtained. If an outcome is better than expected, the prediction error is positive. If the outcome matches expectation, the prediction error approaches zero. If an expected reward fails to occur or the outcome is worse than predicted, the error becomes negative. These differences provide an efficient teaching signal for updating future expectations.

Phasic changes in dopamine activity are strongly associated with this prediction-error principle. Unexpectedly favorable outcomes can produce brief increases in dopaminergic firing, whereas omitted or disappointing rewards can produce decreases relative to baseline activity. Once an outcome becomes fully predicted, the response to the reward itself can diminish while activity appears earlier in response to the cue that predicts it.

This temporal shift is important because it demonstrates that learning changes not only how much value the brain assigns to an outcome, but also when that value becomes represented. A predictive cue acquires significance because it indicates that a future reward is likely. Through repeated experience, increasingly earlier events in a behavioral sequence can become informative, allowing value expectations to propagate backward toward states and actions that precede the final outcome.

This process has a strong conceptual relationship with temporal-difference learning in artificial reinforcement learning. Temporal-difference methods update value estimates whenever the prediction of future reward changes between successive moments. Instead of waiting for the final result, the learner continuously compares current expectations with revised expectations. Dopamine-related prediction-error signals provide a biological parallel to this type of incremental value updating.

Dopamine-producing neurons are concentrated in several midbrain regions, especially the substantia nigra pars compacta and ventral tegmental area. Their projections reach structures including the striatum, prefrontal cortex, limbic regions, and other areas involved in action selection, motivation, learning, and cognitive control. This widespread connectivity allows dopamine to influence many different neural computations rather than operating as a single-purpose reward signal.

The striatum is especially important because it receives cortical information about states, cues, goals, and actions while also receiving dopaminergic signals related to outcomes and expectations. Synaptic changes within corticostriatal circuits can therefore connect a particular situation or action with its learned value. Over repeated experience, these modifications can influence which actions become more likely to be selected in similar contexts.

Dopamine also affects synaptic plasticity. Neural activity alone may indicate which neurons and synapses were involved in an event, but dopamine can influence whether those activity patterns lead to persistent changes. In this sense, local correlations and spike timing can identify candidate connections, while dopamine provides additional information about whether the associated event was valuable, surprising, important, or behaviorally significant.

This interaction can be understood through the idea of three-factor learning. A first factor represents presynaptic activity, a second represents postsynaptic activity, and a third modulatory factor provides information about reward or behavioral significance. Such a framework extends simple Hebbian learning because correlated activity alone is not enough; persistent change can depend on whether a relevant neuromodulatory signal arrives during an appropriate temporal window.

Delayed rewards create an additional difficulty because the behavior responsible for an outcome may have occurred seconds or minutes earlier. Temporary molecular or synaptic states can preserve information about recently active connections until dopamine-related feedback becomes available. These mechanisms are often described computationally using eligibility traces, which allow later reward signals to assign credit selectively to neural events that occurred earlier.

Dopamine is also closely connected with motivation, but motivation should not be reduced to simple pleasure. Dopaminergic systems can influence willingness to expend effort, responsiveness to opportunities, behavioral vigor, and the pursuit of expected outcomes. A highly valued future reward may increase the probability or intensity of action before the reward itself is obtained, illustrating how prediction, motivation, and action preparation interact.

Reward value is also state-dependent. Food may have high value when an organism is hungry and substantially less value after satiety. Similar changes can occur with safety, social interaction, novelty, information, or relief from discomfort. Dopamine-related signals therefore operate within broader systems that incorporate internal physiological states, goals, memories, and environmental context when estimating what an outcome currently means.

Not every dopamine signal should be interpreted as a pure reward prediction error. Dopamine activity can also be influenced by novelty, salience, movement, uncertainty, and task structure, and different dopaminergic populations may show different response patterns. The reward-prediction framework remains extremely influential, but biological dopamine systems are heterogeneous and participate in a wider range of behavioral and cognitive processes.

This diversity is important because real-world learning rarely involves a single objective. Organisms must simultaneously consider energy, safety, uncertainty, social context, immediate opportunities, and long-term goals. Dopaminergic modulation interacts with other neuromodulators and neural circuits to help coordinate behavior under these competing pressures. Reinforcement learning in the brain is therefore distributed rather than controlled by one scalar reward channel.

Prediction errors also support adaptation when environmental conditions change. If a familiar cue stops producing its expected reward, repeated negative prediction errors can reduce the value assigned to that cue or associated action. Conversely, an unexpectedly valuable outcome can quickly increase future expectations. This allows learned behavior to remain sensitive to changing contingencies rather than remaining permanently tied to outdated associations.

From the perspective of artificial intelligence, dopamine-inspired reward prediction provides one of the clearest bridges between neuroscience and reinforcement learning. Concepts such as reward prediction error, temporal-difference updating, value learning, and eligibility traces have close computational counterparts in AI. These parallels have helped researchers formalize biological learning while also inspiring algorithms for agents that improve through interaction and delayed feedback.

However, artificial reward signals are usually much simpler than biological ones. An AI agent may receive a clearly defined numerical reward, whereas the brain integrates external outcomes with internal drives, memory, uncertainty, social signals, and physiological needs. This difference suggests that future adaptive AI may benefit from richer value systems that combine extrinsic reward with intrinsic motivation, novelty, uncertainty reduction, and context-sensitive objectives.

Dopamine and reward prediction therefore form a central mechanism connecting expectation, outcome, plasticity, motivation, and adaptive behavior. Prediction errors indicate when the world is better or worse than expected, dopaminergic modulation helps update relevant neural circuits, and learned values guide future action selection. Together, these processes explain how experience can transform surprising outcomes into improved predictions and more effective behavior over time.

## 03.06 Hippocampal Learning

![](images/image7.png){width="7.268055555555556in" height="7.268055555555556in"}

Hippocampal learning refers to the processes through which the hippocampus rapidly forms, organizes, and retrieves memories of experiences, events, places, and their relationships. Located within the medial temporal lobe, the hippocampus is especially important for episodic and relational memory. Rather than storing isolated facts independently, it helps bind distributed elements of an experience into coherent representations that can later support remembering, inference, navigation, and prediction.

A defining property of hippocampal learning is its ability to acquire information rapidly. A meaningful event may be remembered after only one or a few experiences, unlike many forms of cortical learning that depend on repeated exposure. This rapid encoding allows organisms to remember unusual situations, novel environments, important encounters, and specific episodes without requiring extensive repetition before useful knowledge becomes available.

The hippocampus receives highly processed information from multiple cortical systems through structures of the medial temporal lobe. Visual, spatial, auditory, contextual, and conceptual information can therefore converge within hippocampal circuits. By integrating these distributed inputs, the hippocampus can represent relationships among objects, locations, actions, people, and temporal context, creating memories that preserve the organization of an experience rather than merely its individual components.

Hippocampal circuitry includes interconnected regions such as the dentate gyrus, CA3, and CA1, each contributing differently to information processing and memory formation. Information flows through these circuits while undergoing transformations that support discrimination, association, retrieval, and comparison. The organization of these pathways provides a biological architecture in which related experiences can be connected while similar but distinct experiences remain distinguishable.

Pattern separation is an important function associated with hippocampal learning. Similar experiences can produce overlapping sensory inputs, yet the brain must preserve their differences to avoid confusing one event with another. The dentate gyrus is strongly associated with transforming similar input patterns into more distinct neural representations. This process helps memories retain specificity even when environments, objects, or situations share many common features.

Pattern completion provides a complementary capability. When only part of a previously experienced pattern becomes available, hippocampal circuits can help reconstruct the broader memory associated with it. CA3, with its recurrent connectivity, has frequently been linked to this function. A partial sensory cue, familiar location, or fragment of an event may therefore activate a more complete representation of the original experience.

The combination of pattern separation and pattern completion creates an important computational balance. Memories must remain sufficiently distinct to prevent interference, yet they must also remain accessible from incomplete cues. Excessive separation would make retrieval difficult, while excessive completion could cause similar experiences to become confused. Hippocampal learning continuously manages this tension between preserving specificity and recovering coherent memories.

Synaptic plasticity provides a cellular mechanism supporting hippocampal learning. Long-term potentiation and long-term depression have been extensively studied in hippocampal circuits because changes in synaptic efficacy can preserve relationships established during experience. Coordinated neural activity can strengthen selected pathways, while other patterns can weaken connections, allowing the network to modify its internal organization according to recently encountered information.

The hippocampus is also deeply involved in spatial learning and navigation. Neural populations can represent locations, trajectories, boundaries, directions, and relationships within an environment. Place cells are particularly well known for becoming active when an organism occupies particular locations. Together with interacting navigation systems, these representations support spatial memory, route planning, localization, and flexible reasoning about relationships between places.

Hippocampal representations are not limited to physical space. Similar organizational principles may contribute to representing abstract relationships, conceptual dimensions, sequences, and structured experiences. This suggests that the hippocampus can help construct relational maps that organize information according to how elements are connected. Such representations allow knowledge to support flexible inference beyond direct recall of individual episodes.

Time is another important dimension of hippocampal learning. Episodic memories contain not only information about what happened and where it occurred, but also about the order in which events unfolded. Hippocampal activity can represent temporal context and sequences, helping distinguish events that contain similar components but occur in different orders. This capability contributes to sequence memory, prediction, and reconstruction of past experiences.

The hippocampus also interacts closely with the cortex during memory consolidation. Newly acquired memories may initially depend strongly on hippocampal representations, while repeated reactivation can gradually influence distributed cortical networks. Over longer periods, statistical regularities across experiences can become integrated into more stable cortical knowledge. This cooperation provides a mechanism for combining rapid episodic learning with slower extraction of generalized structure.

Replay is an important process within this hippocampal--cortical interaction. Patterns of neural activity associated with previous experiences can be reactivated during rest, quiet wakefulness, or sleep. Replay may strengthen memories, reorganize representations, and provide cortical systems with repeated opportunities to learn from experiences without requiring the original external events to occur again. It therefore functions as a form of internally generated learning experience.

Replay may also support planning rather than only memory consolidation. Neural sequences can represent possible trajectories or relationships that are not identical to the organism's immediate sensory experience. By internally reactivating or recombining representations, hippocampal systems may contribute to evaluating future possibilities. Memory can therefore become a resource for prediction and decision making rather than functioning only as a record of the past.

Hippocampal learning is strongly influenced by novelty and behavioral significance. New environments, unexpected relationships, and important events may produce stronger encoding than highly familiar information. Neuromodulatory systems can alter hippocampal plasticity according to attention, arousal, reward, and novelty. This enables memory resources to be allocated selectively instead of preserving every encountered event with equal strength and precision.

The hippocampus does not operate independently from reinforcement learning systems. Episodic memories can preserve information about states, actions, contexts, and outcomes, allowing previous experiences to influence future decisions. Interactions among the hippocampus, prefrontal cortex, basal ganglia, and dopaminergic systems can combine memory with value estimation, planning, and action selection, particularly when decisions depend on specific past experiences.

From an artificial intelligence perspective, hippocampal learning provides a biological model for rapid memory acquisition and experience-based adaptation. Conventional neural networks often require many training examples, whereas episodic memory mechanisms can preserve individual experiences after limited exposure. This distinction has inspired approaches involving external memory, memory-augmented neural networks, retrieval-based systems, experience replay, and few-shot learning.

Pattern separation and pattern completion also have direct computational relevance. Artificial systems must store similar experiences without excessive interference while retrieving useful information from partial or noisy queries. Memory architectures that separate representations during storage and reconstruct related information during retrieval can improve robustness. These principles are particularly relevant to continual learning, embodied agents, and systems operating in changing environments.

Hippocampal replay has a notable parallel in artificial experience replay. Reinforcement-learning agents can store previous transitions and later reuse them for additional training, improving data efficiency and reducing correlations between consecutive experiences. Biological replay is substantially richer and should not be treated as identical to this engineering technique, but both illustrate the value of learning repeatedly from stored experience rather than relying exclusively on new observations.

Hippocampal learning therefore provides a bridge between memory, spatial cognition, temporal organization, prediction, and adaptive intelligence. Rapid encoding captures specific experiences, pattern separation protects their distinctiveness, pattern completion supports retrieval from incomplete cues, and replay contributes to consolidation and planning. Through interaction with cortical and reinforcement systems, hippocampal memory transforms individual experiences into resources for future inference, learning, and behavior.

## 03.07 Cortical Learning

![](images/image8.png){width="7.268055555555556in" height="7.268055555555556in"}

Cortical learning refers to the gradual modification of representations and connections within the cerebral cortex as experience accumulates over time. Unlike rapid hippocampal learning, which can encode individual episodes after limited exposure, cortical learning generally extracts recurring statistical structure across many experiences. This slower process allows the brain to develop stable knowledge that generalizes beyond particular events while remaining useful across changing contexts and situations.

The cerebral cortex contains distributed networks that process sensory information, language, concepts, actions, spatial relationships, and higher cognitive functions. Learning modifies these networks rather than placing knowledge into a single storage location. Representations emerge through coordinated activity across populations of neurons, allowing different aspects of an object, event, concept, or action to be encoded across interacting cortical regions.

Repeated experience is particularly important for cortical learning because regularities become visible across many observations. Features that consistently occur together can gradually develop stronger functional relationships, while unreliable associations may remain weak or disappear. Through this process, cortical networks can extract stable patterns from noisy and variable input, transforming individual observations into representations that capture common structure rather than incidental details.

This statistical learning capability contributes directly to generalization. An organism rarely encounters exactly the same sensory input twice, yet it must recognize objects, situations, and relationships despite variations in viewpoint, lighting, location, timing, or context. Cortical learning gradually develops representations that preserve behaviorally important information while becoming less sensitive to irrelevant variation, supporting robust perception and flexible cognition.

Hierarchical processing is another important characteristic of cortical organization. Early sensory regions tend to represent relatively local or elementary properties, while subsequent processing stages integrate information into increasingly complex patterns. Through experience-dependent learning, combinations of simpler features can support representations of objects, categories, relationships, actions, and abstract concepts. This hierarchy provides an important biological analogy to representation learning in deep neural networks.

Cortical learning is supported by synaptic plasticity operating throughout cortical circuits. Repeated patterns of activity can strengthen selected connections, while other connections may weaken through complementary plasticity mechanisms. Hebbian relationships, timing-dependent effects, neuromodulation, and homeostatic regulation can all influence these changes. Learning therefore emerges from the interaction of multiple local mechanisms rather than from a single universal cortical learning rule.

Local plasticity must operate within mechanisms that preserve network stability. If every frequently activated connection continuously increased in strength, cortical activity could become unstable and representations could lose selectivity. Homeostatic plasticity, inhibitory regulation, synaptic competition, and normalization help maintain useful operating ranges. These processes allow cortical networks to remain plastic enough to learn while sufficiently stable to preserve previously acquired structure.

The cortex also contains extensive recurrent and lateral connections. Information does not simply move through a one-way sequence from sensory input to behavioral output. Higher cortical areas can influence lower areas, neighboring populations interact, and recurrent activity can maintain or refine representations over time. Such interactions allow learning to incorporate context, expectations, attention, previous knowledge, and relationships among multiple sensory or conceptual signals.

Prediction provides an important perspective on cortical learning. Neural systems continuously encounter temporal and spatial regularities that make some future inputs more likely than others. By learning these regularities, cortical circuits can form expectations about upcoming sensory events and compare them with actual observations. Differences between prediction and observation can provide informative signals for modifying internal representations and improving future predictions.

This predictive perspective is closely related to self-supervised learning. The natural environment does not provide explicit labels for every visual image, sound, movement, or event, yet sensory streams contain abundant internal structure. Previous observations can help predict later observations, missing information can be inferred from surrounding context, and different sensory modalities can constrain one another. Cortical learning can therefore exploit relationships within experience itself as sources of supervision.

Multimodal integration is especially important because real-world experiences rarely arrive through only one sensory channel. Visual appearance, sound, touch, proprioception, movement, and spatial information can describe different aspects of the same event. Cortical networks learn relationships among these signals, allowing information from one modality to influence interpretation of another. Such integration contributes to coherent perception and increasingly abstract representations of objects and situations.

Cortical learning also interacts strongly with attention. The brain cannot process every available signal with equal depth, so attentional systems prioritize information according to goals, novelty, relevance, and behavioral demands. Attention can alter neural activity and plasticity, making selected information more likely to influence future representations. Learning is therefore shaped not only by what is present in the environment but also by what the organism actively processes.

Neuromodulatory systems further regulate cortical plasticity according to behavioral context. Signals involving acetylcholine, dopamine, norepinephrine, and other modulators can influence learning according to attention, uncertainty, reward, novelty, and arousal. These mechanisms allow similar patterns of cortical activity to produce different amounts of lasting change depending on whether the experience is routine, unexpected, important, or associated with meaningful consequences.

Cortical learning is closely connected with hippocampal learning through memory consolidation. The hippocampus can rapidly encode specific experiences, while cortical networks gradually integrate information across repeated experiences and reactivations. Hippocampal replay can provide additional opportunities for cortical learning by re-presenting patterns associated with earlier events. Over time, this interaction can transform detailed episodic experiences into more generalized and distributed knowledge.

This complementary learning arrangement helps address the stability--plasticity problem. Rapidly changing all cortical representations whenever a new event occurs could damage established knowledge, while extremely slow adaptation would prevent effective learning. A rapidly learning hippocampal system can preserve specific experiences while slower cortical mechanisms extract common structure. Their cooperation enables both fast acquisition of novel information and stable long-term generalization.

Cortical learning is therefore central to semantic knowledge. Repeated experiences with objects, words, actions, places, and relationships allow common properties to become increasingly independent of the particular episodes in which they were encountered. Knowledge can consequently shift from remembering a specific event toward understanding a general concept. This transformation supports categorization, language, reasoning, and the flexible application of previous knowledge to unfamiliar situations.

Experience can also reorganize cortical representations throughout life. Practice may refine sensory discrimination, repeated motor activity can alter representations involved in skilled movement, and changing environments can modify the importance assigned to particular features. Cortical plasticity generally becomes constrained by existing knowledge and biological development, but the adult cortex retains substantial capacity for adaptation, specialization, and learning.

From an artificial intelligence perspective, cortical learning has strong parallels with representation learning in neural networks. Both involve transforming complex inputs into internal features that support recognition, prediction, and decision making. Hierarchical representations, distributed coding, statistical learning, and repeated exposure are particularly relevant. Deep learning captures some of these computational ideas, although biological cortical learning differs fundamentally in its mechanisms and operating conditions.

Artificial neural networks typically rely on explicit optimization objectives and backpropagation, whereas cortical systems learn through local plasticity, recurrent dynamics, neuromodulation, memory interactions, and continuous sensory experience. The cortex also learns while simultaneously performing perception and behavior rather than separating learning completely from deployment. These differences motivate research into continual learning, self-supervised learning, local adaptation, predictive models, and biologically inspired computation.

Cortical learning therefore provides the brain with a mechanism for transforming repeated experience into stable, generalized knowledge. Distributed representations encode recurring structure, hierarchical processing builds increasingly complex abstractions, prediction and multimodal relationships provide learning signals, and hippocampal interaction supports consolidation. Together, these processes allow individual experiences to contribute gradually to concepts, expectations, skills, and knowledge that can guide intelligent behavior across many situations.

## 03.08 Continual Learning and Forgetting [w/Code]

![](images/image9.png){width="7.268055555555556in" height="7.268055555555556in"}

Continual learning in the brain refers to the ability to acquire new knowledge and skills throughout life while preserving previously useful information. Unlike many artificial learning systems that separate training from deployment, biological learning remains active during normal behavior. New experiences continuously modify neural representations, yet existing memories, habits, and competencies must remain sufficiently stable to support reliable perception and action.

This creates the stability--plasticity problem. A system must remain plastic enough to adapt when environments, goals, or relationships change, but stable enough to avoid destroying knowledge that is still useful. Excessive plasticity can cause new learning to overwrite old representations, whereas excessive stability can prevent adaptation. Biological learning therefore depends on mechanisms that regulate when and where neural change should occur.

Forgetting is a natural consequence of continual learning and is not always a failure. Memories can weaken because of interference, reduced relevance, incomplete retrieval, synaptic modification, or competition from newer information. Some forgetting may actually improve adaptation by reducing outdated or redundant representations. The challenge is not to eliminate forgetting entirely, but to preserve important knowledge while allowing less useful information to change or disappear.

Interference occurs when different memories depend on overlapping neural resources. New experiences can modify synaptic connections that previously contributed to older representations, making those memories harder to retrieve. Conversely, strongly established knowledge can interfere with acquisition of new information. These effects show that biological memory is not a set of independent files but a distributed system in which representations share neural circuits and can influence one another.

The hippocampus and cortex help manage this problem through complementary learning rates. The hippocampus can rapidly encode specific experiences, while cortical systems generally integrate information more gradually across repeated exposure. This separation allows novel events to be stored quickly without requiring immediate large-scale modification of established cortical knowledge. Over time, repeated interactions can determine which information becomes more deeply integrated into long-term representations.

Memory consolidation further supports continual learning by stabilizing recently acquired information. Newly formed memories can be reactivated during rest, quiet wakefulness, and sleep, allowing neural patterns to be strengthened or reorganized. Consolidation can reduce dependence on temporary representations and gradually integrate new information with existing knowledge. This process helps the brain learn from experience without continuously rewriting all previously established neural structure.

Replay is particularly important because previous experiences can be internally reactivated after the original event has ended. By revisiting selected patterns, the brain can continue learning from old experiences while also incorporating new ones. Replay may strengthen important memories, support integration across episodes, and reduce interference. This principle has a clear computational connection to experience replay methods used in artificial continual learning and reinforcement learning.

Selective plasticity provides another strategy. Neural systems do not modify all connections equally whenever new information arrives. Plasticity can depend on novelty, attention, reward, uncertainty, behavioral relevance, and neuromodulatory state. Connections involved in important or surprising events may become more modifiable, while established pathways may remain relatively protected. Such selective updating reduces unnecessary changes and helps maintain stable representations.

Synaptic consolidation can also stabilize important connections. Once a pattern has been learned repeatedly or associated with significant outcomes, molecular and structural processes may make some synapses more resistant to later modification. This creates a biological form of parameter protection, in which connections supporting important knowledge become less vulnerable to interference while other connections remain available for learning new information.

Homeostatic plasticity contributes to continual learning by keeping neural activity within useful operating ranges. Repeated strengthening or weakening could otherwise drive networks toward excessive excitation or inactivity. Homeostatic mechanisms adjust synaptic or cellular responsiveness to maintain stability while local learning continues. This regulation allows neural circuits to remain adaptable without losing the dynamic balance required for reliable computation.

Forgetting can also be influenced by retrieval. A memory that is rarely reactivated may become less accessible, whereas repeated recall can strengthen or modify it. Retrieval is therefore not a passive reading process. Reactivated memories can enter a temporary state in which they become susceptible to updating, a process associated with reconsolidation. This allows existing memories to incorporate new information, but it also creates opportunities for distortion or interference.

Reconsolidation illustrates that long-term memory is dynamic rather than permanently fixed. When an old memory is reactivated in a changed context, new information can become integrated with the existing representation. This flexibility allows knowledge to remain relevant as environments change. However, it also means that remembering can alter what is remembered, reinforcing the idea that continual learning and memory maintenance are deeply interconnected processes.

Sleep is an important component of this balance. During sleep, neural activity can reorganize recently acquired information, strengthen selected memories, and reduce interference between overlapping experiences. Different sleep stages may contribute differently to consolidation and abstraction. Although many details remain under investigation, sleep demonstrates that learning continues even when an organism is not actively interacting with the external environment.

Continual learning also depends on abstraction. If every experience were stored independently, memory demands would grow without limit. Cortical systems gradually extract recurring structure across episodes, allowing many related experiences to contribute to generalized concepts. This reduces dependence on exact episodic storage and enables efficient reuse of knowledge. Generalization therefore acts as both a learning mechanism and a way of managing long-term memory complexity.

At the behavioral level, continual learning requires detecting when old knowledge should be reused and when it should be revised. Familiar environments may benefit from stable habits and established representations, while unexpected changes require increased exploration and adaptation. Prediction errors, novelty signals, and uncertainty can indicate that existing models are no longer sufficient, triggering greater plasticity and helping the brain respond to environmental change.

From an artificial intelligence perspective, biological continual learning highlights the limitations of conventional sequential fine-tuning. Neural networks trained on new tasks can experience catastrophic forgetting, where performance on earlier tasks rapidly deteriorates. Biological systems suggest several possible countermeasures, including replay, selective plasticity, protected representations, multiple learning timescales, modularity, memory systems, and context-dependent regulation of updates.

Artificial continual learning methods often reflect these principles through rehearsal, regularization, parameter isolation, external memory, and dynamic architectures. Rehearsal preserves old examples for reuse, regularization discourages changes to important parameters, and modular approaches allocate different resources to different tasks or contexts. None of these mechanisms exactly reproduces the brain, but each addresses aspects of the same stability--plasticity challenge.

For embodied and adaptive AI, continual learning is especially important because real-world environments cannot be fully anticipated during initial training. Robots and autonomous agents encounter new objects, users, tasks, sensor conditions, and operating contexts after deployment. A useful system must therefore adapt without repeatedly retraining from scratch and without sacrificing previously reliable behaviors whenever new experience is incorporated.

Continual learning and forgetting should therefore be understood as complementary aspects of adaptive intelligence. Plasticity enables new learning, consolidation protects useful information, replay reinforces and reorganizes experience, abstraction reduces memory complexity, and selective forgetting removes information that is no longer valuable. Together, these mechanisms allow biological intelligence to remain both stable and adaptable across a lifetime of changing experience.

## 03.09 Biological vs Backpropagation [w/Code]

![](images/image10.png){width="7.268055555555556in" height="7.268055555555556in"}

Biological learning and backpropagation both describe mechanisms by which adaptive systems change internal connections as a consequence of experience, but they operate according to fundamentally different principles. Backpropagation is an engineered optimization algorithm that calculates how parameters should change to reduce a defined error. Biological learning instead emerges from interacting mechanisms such as synaptic plasticity, neuromodulation, local neural activity, memory, prediction, and behavioral feedback.

Backpropagation begins with an explicit objective or loss function that quantifies the difference between a network's output and a desired result. During learning, information moves forward through the network to produce an output, after which error information is propagated backward. The chain rule of calculus determines how strongly each parameter contributed to the error, allowing gradient-based optimization to update many parameters toward improved task performance.

The biological brain does not appear to implement standard backpropagation in this exact form. Individual synapses generally have access to signals available locally, such as presynaptic activity, postsynaptic activity, membrane state, spike timing, and neuromodulatory influences. They do not obviously receive exact derivatives of a global loss function calculated through every downstream neural pathway. This difference creates the biological credit-assignment problem.

Credit assignment asks how a learning system determines which internal components were responsible for success or failure. Backpropagation solves this mathematically by distributing gradients through computational layers. Biological systems must solve related problems using neural mechanisms that operate under physical constraints. Synaptic eligibility traces, feedback pathways, neuromodulation, local prediction errors, and recurrent interactions may all contribute to assigning learning significance to particular neural events.

A second important difference concerns weight transport. Conventional backpropagation calculates backward signals using the same connection weights involved in the forward computation, effectively requiring precise relationships between forward and backward pathways. This requirement is difficult to map directly onto biological neural circuits. The brain contains extensive feedback connections, but anatomical feedback does not simply reproduce a mathematically exact transpose of feedforward connectivity.

Biological learning is also strongly local and event-dependent. Hebbian plasticity can strengthen relationships between correlated neural activities, while spike-timing-dependent plasticity can make changes sensitive to temporal order. Neuromodulators such as dopamine can provide additional information about reward, novelty, uncertainty, or behavioral significance. Learning therefore results from interactions between local neural events and broader contextual signals rather than from a single globally calculated gradient.

Backpropagation, by contrast, can coordinate enormous numbers of parameters toward one optimization objective. A small error at the output can generate structured updates across many earlier layers, allowing representations to become useful for the final task. This global coordination is one reason deep neural networks can learn highly effective hierarchical representations. Purely local learning rules often face greater difficulty coordinating distant components toward complex system-level objectives.

The timing of learning also differs. Artificial neural networks are commonly trained using distinct batches or episodes in which examples are processed, losses are calculated, gradients are accumulated, and parameters are updated. Biological brains learn continuously while simultaneously perceiving and acting. Neural circuits cannot normally stop interacting with the environment while a separate global optimization phase calculates how every synapse should be modified.

Continuous biological learning creates additional requirements for stability. New information must be incorporated without destroying previously useful knowledge. Mechanisms such as complementary hippocampal and cortical learning, replay, consolidation, homeostatic plasticity, selective plasticity, and synaptic stabilization help manage this stability--plasticity balance. Standard sequential backpropagation does not automatically solve this problem and can produce catastrophic forgetting when networks are trained on changing tasks.

Data requirements provide another contrast. Deep learning systems trained through backpropagation can require large datasets and many repeated optimization steps. Humans and animals can sometimes learn important information from very few experiences, particularly when existing knowledge, episodic memory, attention, and prior representations can be reused. Biological learning therefore combines slow statistical learning with mechanisms capable of rapidly storing specific events and adapting behavior from limited evidence.

Biological learning also operates without explicit labels for most experience. Visual scenes, sounds, movements, bodily states, and interactions arrive as continuous streams rather than curated input--target pairs. The brain can exploit temporal continuity, multimodal relationships, prediction, action consequences, and environmental structure as learning signals. This makes biological learning conceptually related to self-supervised, predictive, reinforcement, and embodied learning rather than exclusively to supervised optimization.

Energy and hardware constraints further distinguish the two approaches. Backpropagation on modern hardware can require large numbers of numerical operations, stored activations, memory transfers, and repeated parameter updates. Biological neural systems operate with event-driven spikes, sparse activity, local communication, and highly parallel physical structures. The brain therefore performs learning under constraints very different from those assumed by conventional digital deep-learning hardware.

This does not mean that biological learning is inherently superior to backpropagation. Backpropagation provides an exceptionally effective solution for optimizing differentiable models when objectives and training data can be defined. It has enabled modern systems for vision, language, speech, control, and generative modeling. Biological learning solves a different problem: maintaining adaptive behavior continuously within a changing physical environment while operating under severe energy, memory, and communication constraints.

Likewise, biological plausibility should not be treated as a requirement for useful artificial intelligence. Aircraft do not reproduce bird flight exactly, and artificial neural networks need not reproduce every biological mechanism. Neuroscience is valuable because it reveals alternative computational principles, including local adaptation, sparse processing, multiple memory systems, continual learning, predictive processing, and neuromodulatory control that may complement rather than replace gradient-based optimization.

Several research directions attempt to reduce the gap between local biological learning and global error-driven learning. Feedback alignment investigates whether approximate feedback pathways can support useful learning without exact weight symmetry. Local learning objectives allow different network regions to optimize signals available nearby. Predictive coding approaches explore whether prediction errors distributed through hierarchical circuits could produce learning dynamics with some functional similarities to gradient-based optimization.

Other approaches combine Hebbian or timing-dependent plasticity with modulatory signals. A synapse can use presynaptic and postsynaptic activity as local factors while a reward-related or error-related signal provides a third factor that determines whether modification should persist. Such three-factor learning rules offer a possible bridge between purely local association and behaviorally meaningful adaptation without requiring every synapse to receive an exact global gradient.

Memory systems provide another important bridge. Backpropagation modifies parameters to encode knowledge directly into network weights, whereas biological systems can combine synaptic learning with rapidly accessible episodic memories. Stored experiences can later be replayed, retrieved, recombined, or used to guide current decisions. Artificial systems increasingly use related ideas through replay buffers, retrieval mechanisms, external memory, and memory-augmented architectures.

A useful comparison therefore separates optimization from adaptation. Backpropagation excels at global optimization across differentiable computational structures, while biological learning excels at integrating multiple learning signals across different timescales during ongoing interaction. Future intelligent systems may benefit from combining these strengths rather than choosing one exclusively: global gradient learning for powerful representations and local mechanisms for rapid, continuous, context-sensitive adaptation.

This hybrid perspective is particularly relevant to embodied and physical AI. A robot can be pretrained using large datasets and backpropagation, obtaining strong perception, language, prediction, or control representations before deployment. During operation, local adaptation, episodic memory, reinforcement signals, replay, and selective parameter updates could then allow the system to adjust to new environments without repeatedly retraining its entire model.

Biological learning and backpropagation should therefore be viewed as different solutions to the general problem of learning from experience. Backpropagation provides precise global credit assignment through mathematical gradients, while the brain relies on distributed plasticity, feedback, neuromodulation, prediction, memory, and continual interaction. Understanding both approaches reveals complementary principles that may guide the development of artificial systems that are simultaneously powerful, efficient, stable, and adaptable.
